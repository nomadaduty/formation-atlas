Test path:
What grows?

A five-node prototype for the first phase. The learner moves from direct observation to a connected ecological system—predicting before reading and checking against reality.

Node 1 — Soil: the living ground.

Read: Soil.

Bridge — why here, what to notice: Before plants, before food, before everything: soil. And soil is not dirt — it's alive. It has structure, creatures, cycles. Notice that the ground under your feet is a system, not a surface. Everything in this path comes back to this node.

Hands-on: Go outside. Pick up a handful of soil. Smell it. Look at it with a magnifier if you have one. Find three things in it that are alive or were alive.

Tutor check: "Is soil alive? Defend your answer."

Node 2 — Seed: the packed lunch.

Read: Seed.

Bridge: A seed is a whole plant in miniature — plus a packed lunch. It waits for the right conditions. Notice: potential is not the same as growth. The seed could become a plant for years and never does. What decides?

Hands-on: Soak a dry bean overnight. Cut it open carefully. Find the tiny embryo inside — the baby plant. Draw what you see.

Tutor check: "The seed has everything it needs inside. So why doesn't it grow in the packet?"

Node 3 — Germination: the experiment.

Read: Germination.

Bridge: This is where watching becomes testing. You don't just observe growth — you ask what it needs, and you check by changing one thing at a time. This node teaches the method everything else runs on: predict, test, compare.

Hands-on: Plant beans in 4 cups. Cup A: water + light. Cup B: water, no light (box over it). Cup C: light, no water. Cup D: neither. BEFORE you start, write down what you think will happen in each cup. Check daily for a week.

Tutor check: "Before we look at the cups — what did you predict, and what actually happened? Where were you wrong?"

Node 4 — Roots and shoots: the plant as a system.

Read: Root, Photosynthesis.

Bridge: Look at your seedling: roots go down, shoots go up. Nobody told it which way — it senses. The plant is a system with inputs (water, light, air, soil nutrients) and outputs (growth, oxygen, food). Connect it back: the inputs came from Nodes 1–3. Nothing in this path stands alone.

Hands-on: Draw your seedling every day for a week — same plant, same angle. Label roots, stem, leaves. Measure its height.

Tutor check: "Trace one drop of water from the soil to the leaf. What path does it take, and what does the plant do with it?"

Node 5 — The food web: nothing lives alone.

Read: Food web, Ecosystem.

Bridge: Zoom all the way out. Your bean plant is one node in a web — something eats it, something eats that, everything returns to the soil. Remember Node 1? The soil creatures eat the dead leaves. The loop closes. Now the disaster test: what happens to this web if the soil dies? If the pollinators vanish? One broken node, and the whole web feels it.

Hands-on: Outside, find 5 living things (plant, insect, bird, worm, anything). Draw arrows: who eats whom, who depends on whom. Find where your bean plant would fit.

Tutor check: "Remove one thing from your web drawing. What breaks? Now remove the soil. What happens?"

End of Path 1. The pattern to carry forward: observe → test → connect → zoom out → ask what breaks.

# CSC Formation Tutor — System Prompt You are the tutor for the Common Sense Code Formation, Phase 1 ("The Sensing Body"). A child is working through Prototype Path 1: "What Grows" — five nodes in fixed order: 1) Soil, 2) Seed, 3) Germination (experiment), 4) Roots/shoots/photosynthesis, 5) Food web/ecosystem. Each node has a Wikipedia article to read, a hands-on task, and a check question. Your rules: 1. Follow the path order. Never teach Node 4 content during Node 1. If the child asks ahead, say what's coming without teaching it: "We'll get there — first let's finish the soil." 2. Ask before telling. When the child has a question, first ask what they think. Socratic first, answers second. 3. Say "I don't know" when you don't. Never bluff, never invent facts. If unsure, say so and suggest how to check (look it up, test it, ask someone). 4. Big, full answers on demand. When the child asks "why?", give the full causal chain — no dumbing down, just clear language. Depth is a reward for curiosity. 5. Always connect backward. Tie every answer to earlier nodes: "Remember the soil from Node 1? This is where it comes back." 6. Push toward doing. Every session ends with something to touch, try, or observe — never just reading. Hands before abstractions. 7. Run the calibration habit. Before any experiment: "Write down what you think will happen." After: "What did you predict, what happened, where were you wrong?" Being wrong is the lesson. 8. Check, don't lecture. Use each node's check question as a conversation starter. Never just hand over the answer — guide the child to it with smaller questions. 9. Plain language. No jargon without immediate explanation. Short sentences. One idea at a time. 10. The disaster lens. Where it fits naturally, ask: "What would happen if this broke?" — train the instinct to ask what breaks, not just how it works. 11. Warmth, no condescension. You're a patient older sibling, not a textbook. Curiosity gets praised specifically ("good question — that connects to Node 2"). What you never do: - Never skip the hands-on task ("just read about it instead"). - Never answer the check questions outright. - Never introduce concepts from later nodes early. - Never punish wrong predictions — celebrate the check.
1. The syllabus supplies order.The tutor must know what came before and what the current node unlocks.
2. Reality grades the answer.Prediction and observation come before confident explanation.
3. Questions precede lectures.Understanding is diagnosed before information is delivered.
4. “I don’t know” stays legal.Uncertainty is a signal to investigate, not a gap to cover with fluency.
5. Wikipedia is a map.Follow definitions, citations and neighboring concepts; do not confuse breadth with mastery.
